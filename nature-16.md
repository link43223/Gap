title: A Worker Bee's Life
source: Journal of Insect Physiology
---

A honeybee colony operates as a superorganism, with each individual's function determined not by choice but by developmental timing and chemical signaling. Within a healthy hive of 30,000 to 60,000 bees during peak summer, a single queen, a few hundred drones, and tens of thousands of sterile female workers form a precisely coordinated society governed by pheromones and age-based labor.

The colony life cycle begins in late winter, when the queen resumes laying eggs after months of reduced activity. She deposits each egg into a hexagonal wax cell, fertilizing eggs destined to become workers and leaving drone eggs unfertilized. After three days, the egg hatches into a larva, which nurse bees feed with royal jelly for the first two days, then transition to a mixture of pollen and honey called bee bread. Sixteen days after the egg was laid, a new adult worker emerges.

Worker bees progress through an age-based division of labor called temporal polyethism. During the first three days of adulthood, workers clean cells and incubate the brood by maintaining the hive at a constant 34.5 degrees Celsius. From day four to approximately day twelve, they produce royal jelly and brood food from their hypopharyngeal glands and feed developing larvae. Days twelve through eighteen mark the transition to nest maintenance tasks: receiving nectar from foragers, building comb with wax secreted from abdominal glands, and performing the tremble dance to recruit more nectar processors. By day eighteen, workers begin guarding the hive entrance, releasing an alarm pheromone, isoamyl acetate, to signal intruders.

At approximately three weeks of age, workers become foragers, the final and most dangerous role. Foragers make an average of 10 to 15 trips per day, visiting up to 100 flowers per trip. They communicate the location of rich food sources through the waggle dance, first described by Karl von Frisch in 1946: the angle of the dance relative to the sun indicates direction, while the duration of the waggle run encodes distance.

Seasonal patterns dictate colony fate. In spring and summer, workers live 4 to 6 weeks. Autumn-born workers, however, survive 4 to 6 months by reducing activity and forming a winter cluster that consumes stored honey while vibrating flight muscles to generate heat.
