title: What Is Lightning?
source: Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society
---

Lightning is a massive electrostatic discharge that occurs within thunderstorms, between clouds, or between clouds and the ground. Each strike represents the sudden equalization of a charge imbalance that builds up inside cumulonimbus clouds through a process not yet fully understood by atmospheric physicists.

Within a developing thunderstorm, collisions between ice crystals and soft hail pellets called graupel transfer electric charge. Lighter ice crystals rise to the top of the cloud, carrying positive charge, while heavier graupel falls toward the cloud base, carrying negative charge. This separation creates an electric field that can reach 300 million volts, sufficient to ionize air molecules and form a conductive plasma channel. The stepped leader, a weakly luminous path of ionized air, advances from the cloud toward the ground in 50-meter increments, each step occurring in approximately 1 microsecond. When it connects with a positively charged upward streamer from the ground, the return stroke—the bright flash visible to observers—travels upward at roughly one-third the speed of light, carrying peak currents of 30,000 amperes.

The rapid heating of air along the discharge channel to approximately 30,000 degrees Celsius—five times hotter than the surface of the Sun—causes explosive expansion of air, producing the sound wave known as thunder. Because light travels faster than sound, the delay between seeing a flash and hearing thunder indicates distance: a 3-second delay equals roughly 1 kilometer.

Globally, Earth experiences approximately 44 lightning strikes per second, totaling nearly 1.4 billion flashes per year, according to data from NASA's Lightning Imaging Sensor aboard the Tropical Rainfall Measuring Mission satellite. Strikes are distributed unevenly: Lake Maracaibo in Venezuela receives the world's highest lightning density, with 233 flashes per square kilometer per year, a phenomenon driven by local topography and wind patterns.

Contrary to popular belief, lightning frequently strikes the same place twice. The Empire State Building in New York is struck an estimated 23 times per year. Approximately 90 percent of lightning strikes occur within the clouds themselves and never reach the ground.
