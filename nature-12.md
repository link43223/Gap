title: How Birds Navigate Migration
source: The Royal Society
---

Each year, billions of birds undertake migrations that span continents and oceans, navigating distances that would challenge human explorers without instruments. The Arctic tern travels approximately 70,000 kilometers annually between Greenland and Antarctica, while the bar-tailed godwit holds the record for the longest non-stop flight: a 12,200-kilometer journey across the Pacific Ocean, tracked by satellite telemetry in a 2020 study published in Proceedings of the Royal Society B.

Birds employ a multi-sensory navigation system that integrates at least three primary mechanisms. The first is a magnetic sense, or magnetoreception, which allows birds to detect Earth's magnetic field lines. Research led by biologists at the University of Oldenburg has identified cryptochrome proteins in the retinas of migratory birds such as the European robin. These light-sensitive proteins create a chemical compass: when struck by blue light, cryptochromes produce radical pairs whose electron spin state is influenced by magnetic field orientation, giving the bird a visual representation of north-south axes. This mechanism was experimentally confirmed by subjecting birds to oscillating magnetic fields that disrupted the radical-pair reaction and caused disorientation.

The second mechanism is celestial navigation. Birds observe the position of the sun during the day and use the stars at night. Indigo buntings raised in planetariums and exposed to a rotating star field developed the ability to orient toward the constellation Ursa Major, demonstrating that star patterns are learned, not innate.

The third mechanism, landmark recognition, is used primarily during the final approach to a destination. Studies using GPS trackers attached to ospreys and cuckoos have shown that experienced migrants follow specific topographic features such as river valleys, mountain ridges, and coastlines.

Young birds on their first migration often navigate by innate magnetic information alone. However, as the Cornell Lab of Ornithology documented through 30 years of tracking data, experienced individuals navigate more efficiently, compensating for wind drift and selecting optimal altitudes between 1,000 and 4,000 meters. This suggests that migration ability improves through learning and memory, combining hardwired magnetic sensitivity with accumulated spatial knowledge.
