title: The Amazon Rainforest
source: Nature
---

The Amazon rainforest spans approximately 6.7 million square kilometers across nine South American countries, with 60 percent lying within Brazil. It represents over half of the planet's remaining tropical forests and is widely considered the most biodiverse terrestrial ecosystem on Earth. Current estimates place the Amazon's species count at roughly 10 percent of all known biodiversity, including 40,000 plant species, 1,300 bird species, 3,000 fish species, and 430 mammal species.

Deforestation remains the most critical threat to the Amazon's integrity. Brazil's National Institute for Space Research (INPE) reported that annual deforestation in the Brazilian Amazon reached 13,235 square kilometers in 2021, a 22 percent increase from the previous year and the highest rate since 2006. The primary drivers are cattle ranching, which accounts for approximately 80 percent of cleared land, followed by industrial soybean farming and illegal logging. Forest loss at this scale disrupts regional rainfall patterns: the Amazon generates approximately half of its own precipitation through evapotranspiration, and continued deforestation risks pushing parts of the ecosystem past a tipping point into drier, savanna-like conditions.

The Amazon also functions as a massive carbon reservoir. Its vegetation and soils store an estimated 150 to 200 billion metric tons of carbon. When forests burn or are cleared, this carbon is released into the atmosphere. Recent studies in Nature indicate that parts of the southeastern Amazon have already shifted from carbon sink to carbon source, emitting more carbon dioxide than they absorb.

Indigenous territories cover approximately 30 percent of the Amazon basin, and research consistently demonstrates that these lands have the lowest deforestation rates. A 2020 study by the World Wildlife Fund found that deforestation inside legally recognized indigenous territories was 2.8 times lower than outside them. Indigenous communities manage their forests through traditional ecological knowledge accumulated over millennia, including rotational farming, selective harvesting, and fire management practices that maintain forest structure and biodiversity.
