title: The Fastest Animals
source: Journal of Experimental Biology
---

Speed among animals has evolved repeatedly across multiple phyla, driven by the demands of predation and escape. The fastest recorded speeds across air, land, and sea reveal a remarkable diversity of physiological solutions to the same challenge: accelerating mass against gravity and drag.

The peregrine falcon is the fastest animal overall, reaching measured dive speeds of 389 kilometers per hour during hunting stoops. This velocity is achieved through a streamlined body, stiff feathers that reduce drag, and a specialized respiratory system that maintains oxygen delivery at extreme speeds. A 2005 study in the Journal of Experimental Biology recorded peregrine terminal velocity by fitting captive birds with GPS loggers during controlled dives from 500 meters.

On land, the cheetah holds the sprint record at 112 kilometers per hour over 100 meters, timed by motion cameras in Kenya's Masai Mara. Unlike the falcon's gravitational acceleration, the cheetah generates speed through explosive muscle power. Its flexible spine increases stride length by 30 percent compared to a similarly sized nonspecialist runner, and its semi-retractable claws provide traction like track spikes. The cheetah's oversized nasal passages and lungs allow rapid oxygen intake, but the animal overheats after approximately 400 meters of full sprint and must rest.

In water, the black marlin has been clocked at 132 kilometers per hour, though this figure comes from a disputed 1970s measurement. The more reliably measured sailfish reaches 110 kilometers per hour, propelled by a crescent-shaped tail that minimizes drag through lunate-tail propulsion, a design convergent with tuna and some sharks.

These extreme speeds share common physiological features: enlarged hearts relative to body mass, fast-twitch muscle fibers capable of rapid contraction, and efficient oxygen transport systems. Evolution has solved the equations of motion differently in each medium, but the underlying principle remains the same—specialization for speed comes at a metabolic cost that limits endurance. The adaptations that produce the fastest animals also make them among the most energy-intensive life forms on the planet.
