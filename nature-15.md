title: How Glaciers Shape Landscapes
source: Journal of Glaciology
---

Glaciers are among the most powerful geological agents on Earth, capable of reshaping entire mountain ranges over timescales of thousands to millions of years. These slow-moving rivers of ice, which currently cover approximately 10 percent of Earth's land surface, erode, transport, and deposit rock on a scale unmatched by any other surface process.

Glacial erosion operates through two primary mechanisms. The first, abrasion, occurs when rock fragments embedded in the base of a glacier scrape against the underlying bedrock like sandpaper. As the glacier moves—typically 10 to 300 meters per year for temperate glaciers—these embedded particles grind the bedrock into fine-grained rock flour. Measurements from the Bench Glacier in Alaska show that abrasion rates can reach 5 millimeters per year on soft bedrock, a rate that over 10,000 years would remove 50 meters of rock.

The second mechanism, plucking or quarrying, happens when meltwater penetrates cracks in the bedrock, freezes, and expands. The ice then pulls away loosened blocks of rock, some the size of houses. Plucking is most effective on the downstream side of bedrock obstacles, where pressure differences cause meltwater to refreeze. Together, abrasion and plucking produce distinctive landforms. U-shaped valleys, with their characteristic steep walls and flat floors, result from glacial widening of pre-existing river valleys. Yosemite Valley in California, carved by repeated glaciations over 2 million years, descends from 1,200-meter cliffs to a flat valley floor 900 meters below. Cirques, armchair-shaped depressions at mountain headwalls, form where ice accumulates and rotates in place, deepening the bedrock. When two cirques erode toward each other from opposite sides of a ridge, they create a sharp, knife-edged arête—the Matterhorn's iconic ridge is a classic example.

As glaciers retreat, they leave behind depositional features. Till, an unsorted mixture of clay, sand, and boulders, accumulates as lateral and terminal moraines that mark a glacier's former extent. Much of the fertile agricultural soil of the North American Midwest, deposited 10,000 to 15,000 years ago during the Laurentide Ice Sheet's retreat, originated as glacial till.

Today, 90 percent of the world's glaciers are retreating due to rising global temperatures, a trend documented by the World Glacier Monitoring Service across 5,000 reference glaciers since 1850.
