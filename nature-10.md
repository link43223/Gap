title: Why Leaves Change Color
source: Scientific American
---

Every autumn, deciduous trees across temperate regions produce one of nature's most striking displays: the transformation of green foliage into brilliant shades of red, orange, and yellow. This process, far from random, involves a carefully orchestrated series of biochemical events driven by decreasing day length and lower temperatures.

During the growing season, leaves appear green because chloroplasts within leaf cells are packed with chlorophyll, the pigment that absorbs red and blue wavelengths from sunlight and converts them into chemical energy through photosynthesis. Chlorophyll concentrations are so high that they mask other pigments present in the leaf. As autumn approaches and daylight shortens, trees receive the signal to prepare for winter dormancy. They begin breaking down chlorophyll and reabsorbing its nitrogen and magnesium components for storage in branches and roots.

Once chlorophyll degrades, two other pigment classes become visible. Carotenoids—the same compounds that color carrots and corn—produce yellow and orange hues. These fat-soluble pigments reside in the chloroplast membrane throughout the growing season but remain hidden behind chlorophyll's dominant green. Unlike chlorophyll, carotenoids are relatively stable in cool temperatures and persist as the green fades.

Red and purple colors result from anthocyanins, water-soluble pigments synthesized in vacuoles during autumn. Unlike carotenoids, anthocyanins are produced only in autumn, not present year-round. Research published by plant physiologists at the University of Vermont indicates that anthocyanins act as a sunscreen, protecting leaf cells from excess light during the chlorophyll-reabsorption process. High sugar content and bright light promote anthocyanin production, which explains why the most vivid reds occur during sunny, cool autumns.

The timing and intensity of color change vary by species and geography. Sugar maples in New England, at elevations between 300 and 900 meters, produce some of the most intense displays when autumn temperatures range between 0 and 7 degrees Celsius. By understanding the pigment chemistry behind autumn color, scientists can also track shifts in seasonal timing linked to climate change.
