How Do Batteries Store Energy?. Batteries store energy through electrochemical reactions that convert chemical potential energy into electrical energy. Every battery cell contains three fundamental components: a cathode (positive electrode), an anode (negative electrode), and an electrolyte — an ion-conducting medium separating the two. During discharge, oxidation occurs at the anode, releasing electrons that travel through an external circuit to the cathode, where reduction takes place. This electron flow constitutes electric current. The voltage of a cell is determined by the difference in electrochemical potential between the two electrode materials, governed by the Nernst equation. Primary (non-rechargeable) alkaline batteries utilize zinc as the anode and manganese dioxide as the cathode, producing approximately 1.5 volts. Secondary (rechargeable) lithium-ion batteries, commercialized by Sony in 1991, operate through intercalation — lithium ions move between layered oxide cathodes (typically LiCoO2) and graphite anodes during charge and discharge cycles. Modern Li-ion cells achieve energy densities of 250-300 Wh/kg, compared to roughly 100 Wh/kg for nickel-metal hydride and 40 Wh/kg for lead-acid batteries. Current research focuses on solid-state batteries, which replace flammable liquid electrolytes with ceramic or polymer solid electrolytes. Such designs could theoretically achieve 500 Wh/kg while eliminating thermal runaway risks. Sodium-ion batteries, which use more abundant and cheaper raw materials, entered commercial production in 2023 as a lower-cost alternative for grid storage. Lithium-sulfur batteries, another emerging technology, promise 500-600 Wh/kg but face challenges with polysulfide dissolution during cycling. The fundamental challenge across all battery chemistries remains balancing energy density, power output, cycle life, manufacturing cost, and safety.
