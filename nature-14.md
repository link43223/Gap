title: The Secret World of Fungi
source: Current Biology
---

Fungi constitute one of the most ancient and diverse kingdoms of life, with an estimated 2.2 million to 3.8 million species globally, of which only approximately 148,000 have been formally described by mycologists. Far from being simple decomposers, fungi form complex networks that regulate nutrient cycles, support plant health, and produce some of the most potent medicinal compounds known to science.

The vegetative body of most fungi consists of mycelium: a network of microscopic tubular threads called hyphae that extend through soil, wood, or other substrates. A single cubic centimeter of soil can contain up to 1 kilometer of hyphae. Through these networks, fungi secrete enzymes that break down complex organic matter such as cellulose and lignin—the two most abundant organic polymers on Earth. Without fungal decomposition, dead plant material would accumulate, and the carbon cycle would grind to a halt. Research published in Fungal Ecology estimates that fungi decompose approximately 70 percent of global terrestrial biomass annually.

Mycelial networks also form symbiotic associations called mycorrhizae with approximately 90 percent of land plants. In this relationship, fungi deliver water, nitrogen, and phosphorus to plant roots in exchange for carbohydrates produced through photosynthesis. A 2019 study in Nature Communications demonstrated that mycelial networks can interconnect multiple plants, creating a "wood wide web" through which trees transfer carbon, nutrients, and chemical warning signals. Paper birch and Douglas fir trees in North American forests were shown to exchange carbon through shared mycorrhizal networks, with older, larger trees acting as hubs.

Medicinally, fungi have transformed human health. Penicillin, derived from the mold Penicillium chrysogenum, became the first antibiotic in 1928 and has saved an estimated 200 million lives. The cholesterol-lowering statins were originally isolated from the fungus Aspergillus terreus. In modern oncology, the immunomodulator lentinan, extracted from shiitake mushrooms, is used as an adjunct therapy for gastric cancer in Japan, while the compound psilocybin from Psilocybe species is under clinical investigation for treatment-resistant depression.
